The wait for answers: can a new UQ screening tool ease the uncertainty of suspected endometriosis?

As Australian research highlights the psychological toll of suspected endometriosis, a new University of Queensland screening tool could help identify people at risk earlier.

By Matilda Hockey

When Evie Smith prepared for surgery after years of severe period pain, she was frightened the doctors would find nothing.

For Smith, the possibility of leaving hospital without an explanation meant continuing to question symptoms that had affected her since her first period at 12.

Endometriosis affects an estimated one in six Queensland women by their early 40s, while diagnosis can take an average of six to eight years in Australia.

For women living with unexplained symptoms, that wait can mean more than managing pain. It can also mean questioning whether their experiences are normal and whether they will be taken seriously.

A 2026 Australian study has drawn attention to the psychological toll of suspected endometriosis, while University of Queensland (UQ) researchers have developed the SAFE score, a screening tool designed to identify women at increased risk and prompt further investigation.

If the tool can help recognise risk earlier, it could offer a new way to address the diagnostic gap and easing the psychological uncertainty of waiting for an answer.

1 dot = 1,000 Queensland women estimated to have been diagnosed with endometriosis by their early to mid-40s.

How many more could be living without answers?

“You’re so scared that they’re going to go in and find nothing and all your answers will just be nothingness,” Smith said.

Smith, 21, said she had experienced severe period pain since her first period at 12.

During high school, she tried different hormonal contraceptives, but her symptoms continued.

After debilitating pain during a holiday in April 2025, she decided to pursue further investigation and surgery in September that year confirmed endometriosis.

For Smith, the diagnosis opened up treatment options and helped her access university accommodations, including extensions, separate exam arrangements, and permission to use heat packs during exams.

“Being able to put a name to it and get accommodations around it is so good,” she said.

For Smith, finally receiving an explanation changed how she understood years of pain.

But for other women, the uncertainty continues.

Sometimes that uncertainty begins because they do not recognise their symptoms as something that warrants investigation.

When symptoms feel normal

For 24-year-old Sophie Johnston, the uncertainty began with an assumption that her symptoms were simply part of having a period.

She experienced irregular periods and severe cramping from her first period at 16, but did not initially question whether they were unusual.

“I always just thought it was normal and I never talked to anyone about it,” Johnston said.

A conversation with a colleague eventually introduced her to endometriosis as a possible explanation for her symptoms.

“If my friend never said something about it, I probably would still have no idea,” she said.

Johnston’s experience highlights how uncertainty can begin before someone seeks medical help.

If symptoms seem normal, there may be little reason to look for an explanation.

A study published in the Journal of Psychosomatic Research in July 2026 examined psychological distress, health-related quality of life and pain among Australians with confirmed or suspected endometriosis.

Researchers surveyed 653 participants, including 550 with a confirmed diagnosis and 103 who suspected they had the condition.

The suspected endometriosis group reported significantly greater anxiety and pelvic pain symptoms than the diagnosed group.

A separate study published in the Medical Journal of Australia found that depression and anxiety were also recorded among women diagnosed with endometriosis, alongside physical symptoms.

The findings draw attention to the experiences of women who are living with symptoms but have not yet received a confirmed diagnosis.

For 21-year-old Laura Cusack, pain and exhaustion affected her schoolwork and everyday life.

Severe cramps sometimes left her spending two days a month in bed, while heavy bleeding contributed to low iron levels.

Although her mother had endometriosis and recognised a possible connection, finding a treatment that worked took time.

“The biggest thing for me was just being so exhausted and just unsure,” Cusack said.

What a diagnosis can change

Brisbane gynaecologist Dr Melinda Heywood said the experience of seeking a diagnosis could be shaped by how women's pain is understood and treated.

“Women’s symptoms can be written off as anxiety or hysteria,” she said.
“Repeatedly being told that symptoms are normal can leave patients questioning their own experiences.”

Some arrive at specialist appointments feeling they must fight to have their concerns investigated.

Heywood said she believed a diagnosis could provide important relief for people who had spent years searching for an explanation.

“When someone has a laparoscopy and wakes up to be told they have endometriosis, their overwhelming feeling can be relief,” she said.

A diagnosis can give patients an explanation for their pain, help them communicate their needs and open pathways to treatment and practical support.

For Smith, receiving a diagnosis helped her recognise that the pain she had experienced was not something she had simply exaggerated or needed to tolerate.

For 22-year-old Charlee Webb, however, the difficulty has been deciding whether her symptoms warrant further investigation at all.

Webb has experienced irregular periods, heavy bleeding, and severe cramps, but said comparing herself with other women sometimes led her to minimise what she was experiencing.

“We could have the worst period of our life and go, ‘Oh, well, it’s like that for everyone,’” she said.

The time, money, and effort involved in seeking a diagnosis have also influenced her decision to wait while her symptoms remain manageable.

Her experience shows how uncertainty can influence decisions about healthcare when someone is unsure whether their symptoms are serious enough to investigate.

It also raises the question of whether earlier recognition could help prompt further assessment.

Could earlier recognition help?

For Dr Sally Mortlock, a member of the UQ research team behind the SAFE score, one challenge is recognising symptoms early enough to prompt investigation.

“Our goal with the SAFE score was to try and address that lag between the first experience of symptoms and then the actual diagnosis,” she said.

The SAFE score was developed using data from more than 9,000 women in the Australian Longitudinal Study on Women's Health.

Researchers examined symptoms experienced across adolescence and adulthood and identified six factors associated with a later endometriosis diagnosis.

The researchers found that symptoms experienced during adolescence, particularly between the ages of 16 and 19, were especially useful in predicting a later diagnosis.

The resulting score is intended to help general practitioners identify people who may be at increased risk and guide decisions about further assessment.

Mortlock said earlier recognition could help patients and GPs identify symptoms that warrant investigation rather than dismissing them as ordinary period pain.

“Maybe if GPs or even the girls themselves are aware of that, they would go and get it investigated and can start this diagnostic pathway a lot sooner,” she said.

The research has been published, but further development is needed before the score can be routinely used in general practice.

“We’re on the pathway where we’re trying to translate it into GP clinics but we’re looking for funding to do it”, Mortlock said.

The team is currently consulting consumer and clinician groups to determine how the tool should work in clinical settings.

While SAFE could help identify people at increased risk, further work will be needed to establish how it performs in practice and whether earlier recognition changes the diagnostic journey.

The wait continues

For Smith, an earlier explanation might have changed how she understood her symptoms as a teenager.

Her diagnosis eventually provided an explanation and access to university support.

But the experiences of Johnston, Cusack and Webb show that the path towards answers is different for everyone.

Some people discover possible explanations through conversations with friends, while others spend years trying to find effective treatment or remain unsure whether further investigation is worthwhile.

A screening tool cannot remove every barrier to diagnosis or guarantee that someone's symptoms will be explained.

But SAFE could offer clinicians another way to recognise patterns of symptoms earlier and prompt the next step in the diagnostic process.

For women waiting for answers, that possibility matters because the wait is not simply time spent without a diagnosis.

It can also mean questioning whether their experiences are normal, whether their pain deserves attention and how to explain something they still do not fully understand.

Smith's experience offers a reminder of what an answer can mean after years of uncertainty.

“Just because something is common doesn’t mean it’s normal,” she said.
