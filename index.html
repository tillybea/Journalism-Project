Can a new UQ screening tool ease the uncertainty of suspected endometriosis?

As researchers work to identify endometriosis risk earlier, people living with unexplained symptoms describe what it means to wait for an answer — and what a diagnosis can change.

A long-form journalism project · By Matilda Hockey

↓ Scroll to explore the story
01 / The human story

The fear of finding nothing

When Evie Smith prepared for surgery after years of severe period pain, she was frightened the doctors would find nothing.

For Smith, the possibility of leaving hospital without an explanation meant continuing to question symptoms that had affected her since her first period at 12.

Why this story matters

Endometriosis affects an estimated one in six Queensland women by their early 40s, while diagnosis can take years. For people living with unexplained symptoms, the wait can mean more than managing pain: it can also mean questioning whether their experiences are normal and whether they will be taken seriously.

A 2026 Australian study found that people who suspected they had endometriosis reported greater anxiety symptoms and pelvic pain than those with a confirmed diagnosis. Meanwhile, UQ researchers have developed the SAFE score, a screening tool designed to identify people at increased risk and prompt further investigation. The team is seeking funding to translate the research into a practical tool that can be evaluated in clinical settings. Whether earlier identification could ease psychological distress remains untested.

02 / Evie's story

When the answer finally came

“You’re so scared that they’re going to go in and find nothing and all your answers will just be nothingness.”Evie Smith

Smith said her symptoms began with her first period at 12. She tried different hormonal contraceptives through high school, but her pain continued. After debilitating pain during a holiday in April 2025, she decided to pursue further investigation.

In September 2025, surgery confirmed endometriosis and adenomyosis. The diagnosis helped Smith access treatment and a university student access plan, including extensions, separate exam arrangements and permission to use heat packs during exams.

“Being able to put a name to it and get accommodations around it is so good.”Evie Smith

Smith said diagnosis also changed how she looked back on her teenage years. As she began talking with friends, she realised not everyone experienced pain that disrupted school or work. The experience raises a question beyond how long diagnosis takes: what happens while people try to understand symptoms without knowing their cause?

03 / The diagnostic journey

Years can pass between symptoms and answers.

A diagnostic journey rarely follows a neat, predictable line. Explore the milestones in Smith's experience, then consider how a different path can look for someone still seeking answers.

6–8 years · commonly reported Australian diagnostic delay

Diagnostic-delay estimates vary depending on the study and how the starting point is defined. One Australian survey reported a 12.3-year average among its 506 participants with self-reported endometriosis; that finding is specific to that sample, not a universal or Queensland-specific timeline.

04 / When symptoms feel normal

Not everyone knows what to look for.

Sophie Johnston experienced irregular periods and severe cramping from her first period at 16, but initially assumed her symptoms were normal.

“I always just thought it was normal and I never talked to anyone about it.”Sophie Johnston

A conversation with a colleague introduced Johnston to endometriosis as a possible explanation.

“If my friend never said something about it, I probably would still have no idea.”Sophie Johnston

Laura Cusack described severe cramps that sometimes left her spending two days a month in bed. Heavy bleeding contributed to low iron levels, while pain and exhaustion affected her schoolwork.

“The biggest thing for me was just being so exhausted. And just unsure.”Laura Cusack
05 / Still seeking clarity

When is it worth seeking answers?

Charlee Webb has experienced irregular periods, heavy bleeding and severe cramps but has not pursued further specialist investigation. She said comparing herself with other women sometimes leads her to minimise her symptoms.

“We could have the worst period of our life and go, ‘Oh, well, everyone … it’s like that.’”Charlee Webb

The time, money and effort involved in seeking a diagnosis have also influenced her decision to wait while her symptoms remain manageable. Her experience does not establish that she has endometriosis. It shows how uncertainty can shape healthcare decisions when someone is unsure whether their symptoms are serious enough to investigate.

Persistent or disruptive pelvic pain and menstrual symptoms can have a range of causes. This story is not a diagnostic guide; people with concerns should discuss their symptoms with a qualified healthcare professional.

06 / The value of a diagnosis

Sometimes, a name changes the story.

“For some women, though, having a label is important: they want validation and to know there is a clear cause for their pain.”Dr Melinda Heywood · Brisbane gynaecologist

Heywood said the experience of seeking a diagnosis can be shaped by how women's pain is understood and treated. People who have repeatedly been told their symptoms are normal may arrive at specialist appointments feeling they must fight to have their concerns investigated.

A diagnosis can offer an explanation for pain, help patients communicate their needs and open pathways to treatment and practical support. However, Heywood cautioned against assuming that everyone with pelvic pain has endometriosis or that surgery is always necessary before treatment can begin. Pelvic pain can have multiple causes, and management can involve several approaches.

07 / The psychological impact

The symptoms are not only physical.

A 2026 Australian study in the Journal of Psychosomatic Research examined psychological distress, health-related quality of life and pain among people with diagnosed or suspected endometriosis.

It surveyed 653 participants: 550 reported a confirmed diagnosis and 103 suspected they had the condition. The suspected group reported significantly greater pelvic pain and anxiety symptoms.

The study was cross-sectional, so it cannot establish that diagnostic uncertainty causes anxiety or that receiving a diagnosis will necessarily resolve it. Pain, access to healthcare and other circumstances may also affect wellbeing.

A separate 2025 Australian study, “A name to the pain”, found that 96.2 per cent of participants with endometriosis considered diagnosis important. Participants described validation, legitimacy and relief from uncertainty.

These studies describe their own samples. Their findings should not be interpreted as proof that diagnosis alone prevents psychological distress.

08 / What the records show

Symptoms can be recorded long before diagnosis.

An Australian study of general-practice records looked at symptoms documented before an endometriosis diagnosis among 19,786 women aged 14–49. These records do not capture every symptom experienced or everyone who suspects they have the condition, but they provide a picture of what appeared in participating practices.

09 / Inside SAFE

Six factors. A possible earlier signal.

The SAFE score was developed from data on more than 9,000 women in the Australian Longitudinal Study on Women's Health. Researchers identified six factors associated with a later endometriosis diagnosis, with the strongest predictive performance for symptoms experienced during adolescence, particularly ages 16–19.

“Our goal with the SAFE score was to try and address that lag between the first experience of symptoms and then the actual diagnosis.”Dr Sally Mortlock · UQ research team

Mortlock said the score is intended to help flag when further assessment may be appropriate, not to confirm endometriosis. The team is seeking funding and consulting consumer and clinician groups on how the research could become a practical clinic-facing tool.

10 / From research to real-world care

Funding the next step.

The SAFE research has been published, but translating the model into a practical tool will require further development and clinical evaluation. Mortlock said the team is seeking funding and working with consumer and clinician groups to determine how the tool should be presented and interpreted.

“We're looking for funding to do it.”Dr Sally Mortlock · UQ research team

A funding pathway could support development of the clinic-facing tool, testing in general practice and evaluation of whether earlier identification improves referral decisions and patient experience. The psychological benefit should be measured rather than assumed.

What needs to happen next?

Translate the published model into a clinic-facing tool with input from patients and clinicians.

Evaluate whether the tool can support GPs to recognise symptoms, decide on follow-up and refer people appropriately. Test usability and performance before wider implementation.

Help young people, families and health professionals understand which symptoms warrant medical attention, while ensuring people can access appropriate care and support.

Circle back

Earlier recognition needs more than a research paper.

For Smith, diagnosis changed how she understood years of pain. Looking back, she said greater awareness could have helped her recognise earlier that what she was experiencing was not something she simply had to tolerate.

“Just because something is common doesn’t mean it’s normal.”Evie Smith

That message needs to reach beyond individual patients. Young people need clear information about symptoms that warrant medical attention, and healthcare professionals need tools and support to respond when those symptoms are raised.

Funding is the immediate next step for the SAFE research team: to develop the tool, test it in clinical settings and establish whether it can help people reach appropriate care sooner. Alongside that work, greater awareness can help prevent concerning symptoms from being normalised or ignored.

“What they're experiencing is a real issue. It's not just in their head. It's not just imaginary.”Dr Sally Mortlock · UQ research team

The SAFE score has not yet been shown to ease the psychological effects of diagnostic uncertainty. Funding its next stages would allow researchers to test whether earlier recognition can make a difference — while awareness can help ensure young people know their pain deserves to be taken seriously.
